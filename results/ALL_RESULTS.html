Rebuilding weekly dengue curves from annual totals

Every model and experiment run so far, with plain explanations of what each model does and why it was included. 2024 has never been loaded; it stays locked as the final test.

BR 300 municipalities, 27 states, 2010–2023 MX 32 states, 2017–2023 Primary score: JSD (lower is better)

Summary

  • Climate barely helps. Local weather lowers the error by about 1–3 %, and the El Niño index (ONI) makes it worse. The pre-registered climate hypotheses H1, H2 and H3 are not supported (Section 3).
  • Knowing the size of the year matters. Letting the shape depend on how big the year is (B4s vs B4) lowers the error by about 5–7 %, more in epidemic years.
  • Same-year Google Trends is the strongest usable signal. Adding state-level searches for “dengue” lowers the error by 12.7 % in Brazil, and it is better in every one of the 12 test years (Section 6). It also helps from only two years of training data (Section 8).
  • Trends also improves the base paper's final model. Scaled INLA + Trends is about 7 % better than scaled INLA in both countries, in every test year (Sections 9 and 12). Weather on top adds almost nothing.
  • Mexico gives the same ranking on far less data (7 years, 32 states): Trends and the regional curve help, climate does not. The gains are smaller (3–8 %) and less certain.
  • Best usable models: in Brazil, B4s + Trends (S2) has the lowest error. In Mexico, INLA + Trends + weather and S2 tie (both 0.0815 JSD).

The task and the scores

Many places only report dengue as yearly totals. The task is to split a known yearly total N into weekly counts. Each model predicts what share of N falls in each epidemiological week. The counts are then rounded so they add up exactly to N. We test this on places and years where the true weekly counts are known, hiding them from the model.

ScoreWhat it measuresGood value
JSD (Jensen–Shannon divergence)How different the rebuilt weekly shape is from the true one. The main score.0 = identical. Pure random noise alone gives about 0.015 (Brazil) or 0.010 (Mexico), so that is the best reachable.
Peak-week errorWeeks between the true and the rebuilt peak week.Lower is better
Onset errorWeeks between the true and rebuilt week in which 10 % of the year's cases are reached.Lower is better
EMDOn average, how many weeks the cases have to be moved to turn the rebuilt curve into the true one.Lower is better
MAE counts, 80 % coverage, CRPSError in weekly case counts, and how honest the uncertainty bands are (80 % of true weeks should fall in the 80 % band).Coverage near 0.80

“Change %” in the tables means the relative change in mean JSD against the stated comparator; negative means less error. A difference is called clearly better or clearly worse only if two bootstrap checks both agree: one resamples places, the other resamples test years. Otherwise it is not clear.

How models are tested

DesignHow it worksWhy
A Expanding windowTrain on 2010 up to the year before, test the next year. 12 test years (2012–2023).The pre-registered primary design. Like real use: only the past is known.
B Learning curveFixed test years (2019, 2021, 2023), trained on the k years just before them.Shows how many years of history each model needs. Important for countries with short records.
C Leave one year outHide one year, train on all others (including later years). 14 folds in Brazil, 7 in Mexico.Uses the most data. Checks that design-A findings hold. The only design possible for Mexico's 7 years.

In every design the hidden year's weekly data are removed before any feature is computed. An automatic leakage audit re-runs the feature code with the hidden year replaced by random numbers and checks nothing changes. Every fold of every run passed it.

Every model, explained

All models answer the same question: what share of this year's cases falls in each week? They differ in what information they use.

Baselines

B0

Uniform: every week gets the same share.

The worst sensible answer. Any useful model must beat it by far.

B1

Seasonal profile: the average share of each week over the training years of that place.

The obvious simple method. Shows how much a fitted model adds over plain averaging.

B1p

B1 using past years only (pilot).

Measures how much “future” training years in leave-one-year-out make results look better.

B4

The base paper's Dirichlet-multinomial (DM) model (Lim et al., 2026): national, regional and local seasonal terms.

The published method we start from. Its weak point: every year of a place gets the same shape, so early or late epidemics are missed.

B4s

B4 plus terms that let the shape depend on how big the year is (log of the annual total).

Big epidemics tend to peak more sharply. The annual total is known in this task, so using it is fair. B4s is the main comparator for every new model.

Climate models

M1

B4s + this year's weather anomalies (temperature, rainfall, humidity; lags of 0–20 weeks in 5 blocks) + the El Niño index (ONI).

The main hypothesis (H1): weather tells when this year's epidemic happens.

M1-noONI

M1 without ONI: local weather only.

Separates local weather from the global El Niño signal.

M1-short

M1 with only lags of 0–3 weeks.

Tests H2: do long lags (mosquito breeding time) matter?

M1-mult

M1 with a multinomial instead of a DM likelihood (pilot).

Checks that the DM's extra spread is needed.

M2

Boosted trees (XGBoost) with a softmax, using the same weather features.

Checks whether a flexible machine-learning model finds more in the weather than the DM.

M1-shufTT
M1-shufTest

M1 refitted with weather shuffled between years / M1 given the wrong year's weather at test time.

Controls. If climate gains were real, shuffled weather should lose them.

M1-R
M1-RTH

Rain-lag follow-up: lags to 26 weeks; rain (or all three variables) split into wet and dry anomalies.

Tests whether a richer rain description rescues climate.

INLA models (the base paper's final method)

INLA-scaled

The Bayesian model the base paper chose: a smooth seasonal curve shared across places, regional and local deviations, and week-to-week carry-over. Its predicted counts are rescaled to the known annual total.

The strongest published competitor. Any new method should be compared to it.

INLA-unscaled

The same fit without rescaling.

Same shape as scaled (identical JSD), but counts that do not add up; shows why scaling matters for count errors.

INLA-seasonal-only

Only the seasonal part of the same fit.

Diagnostic: how much the rest of the model adds.

+clim15 / +clim20

INLA-scaled plus 15 local weather features, or 20 with ONI (Brazil, design A).

Same climate question, asked of the base paper's model.

INLA-scaled-trends

INLA-scaled plus the same Google Trends features as S2.

Tests whether the Trends gain is specific to the DM or works in any model.

…-clim15trends

INLA-scaled plus Trends plus 15 weather features.

Does weather add anything once Trends is in?

Same-year timing models (extension)

S2

B4s + Google Trends: weekly search interest for “dengue” in the state that year, as anomalies against training years (lags 0–3 weeks).

Searches rise and fall with the actual epidemic of that year, and they exist even where case data are missing. This is the main usable timing model.

S2C / S2C-noONI

S2 + weather, with or without ONI.

Checks whether climate adds anything on top of Trends.

T1

The seasonal shape of B4s, shifted earlier or later by the timing shift of the state curve that year.

The simplest possible use of timing: only a shift, no shape change.

S1

B4s + the weekly curve of the other places in the same state (Brazil) or region (Mexico) that year, with the target left out.

An upper bound: it needs the neighbours' weekly counts for the same year, which a truly missing year usually does not have.

S3 / S3C

S1 + Trends; S3C adds weather and ONI.

Upper bounds: how far all signals together can go.

S1-p50 / p25 / ns

S1 built from a random 50 % or 25 % of the state's towns, or only from towns outside the study.

How well the state curve works when fewer towns report.

S2-wy, S1-wy, S3-wyTest

Fitted with another year's searches or state curve, or given the wrong year only at test time.

Controls: if the gain disappears with the wrong year, it comes from real timing, not from extra model flexibility.

1. Reproducing the base paper done

We re-implemented the base paper's DM model and ran it on their own cross-validation data (3,648 country-months).

MAERMSE80 % cov.CRPS
Published DM13.478.50.959.6
Our reproduction (NUTS)13.3478.10.9489.53

Result: reproduced (MAE within 0.5 %). Two facts motivated everything after: the DM's point error is no better than simple historical shares, and every held-out year of a country gets an identical shape (differences below 1e-16). The model cannot tell an early year from a late one.

10. Mexico: data MX

  • Cases: OpenDengue V1.3.1, weekly by state (source WHO/PAHO, all reported cases). Checked against the official DGE case-level open data for 2020–2022: yearly totals within 3 %, weekly correlation ≥ 0.98, and 83–99 % of state-weeks exactly identical. No discrepancy, so OpenDengue was used.
  • Gaps filled from DGE with the same case definition: 2020 week 53, 2021 weeks 50–52, 2022 weeks 33–51, and all of 2023.
  • Usable span: 2017–2023 only (earlier years use a different case definition or are missing). So 7 leave-one-year-out folds.
  • Units: the 32 states take the place of municipalities. 31 states have at least one year with ≥ 100 cases and are scored: 184 state-years.
  • Weather: NASA POWER at each state capital. Trends: every state has its own series. “State curve” (S1) becomes the summed curve of the other states in the same region (5 regions, 3–7 states each).

Limitations

  • Exploratory. The climate study (Section 3) was pre-registered. The Trends, timing and Mexico work came after its results were known, so it is exploratory. The locked 2024 test year and other countries are the real out-of-sample checks.
  • Upper bounds. S1, S3 and S3C use other places' weekly counts for the same year. They show what is possible if neighbours report, not a usable method where a year is fully missing.
  • Google Trends starts in 2004 and is sparse in small states (14 Mexican state-years are all zeros). The Trends gain is smallest in the North of Brazil and in low-search states.
  • Mexico is small: 7 years, 184 scored state-years, weather taken at the capital (dengue is often in lowland cities away from highland capitals).
  • Notified cases are not infections; 2020 has COVID-era reporting effects (removing it changes no conclusion in Brazil).
  • Timing is improved, not solved: peak-week error is still about 5–6 weeks on average.
